# The choices we can make, and how to get ready

*A plain-language companion to the Century Superforecaster model. It takes the handful of social and political choices the model says a real actor could make today, explains each one in everyday terms, shows how you would put it into practice, and is honest about what gets in the way. It then sketches the foundations of a practical readiness system: a way for governments, companies and other institutions to measure how prepared they really are before powerful AI arrives.*

---

## Part 1: Why this document exists

The model behind this paper plays out the next hundred years, from 2026 to 2126, across 800,000 imagined versions of the world. Each version makes slightly different guesses about how fast AI improves, how likely wars and pandemics are, and how well governments cope. At the end it counts how the worlds turned out: how many ended well, how many ended badly, and, the useful part, which choices moved those numbers.

The headline leans the wrong way. With no special effort, about **39 in 100** worlds end broadly well and about **49 in 100** end irreversibly badly. The single most common ending is neither extinction nor utopia. It is a quiet loss of control: humans survive, often comfortably, but no longer steer their own future. The model calls this **disempowerment**, and it happens in roughly **33 in 100** worlds.

Those figures assume that the work of testing and containing an AI system goes stale as the system gets stronger, which it does: a test written for last year's model is weaker evidence about this year's. Nobody has measured how fast that happens, so the model also reports the century on the opposite assumption, that containment holds and nothing goes stale. On that reading it is **44 good in 100 against 42 bad**, a coin flip. The truth is somewhere between, and this document uses the first reading throughout.

Here is the central finding, and it survives either reading. If you make only the social and political choices a real actor can already reach, such as sharing the gains, building institutions that react, funding safety work and cooperating instead of racing, and you leave the pace of the technology completely untouched, the share of good worlds rises from **39 to 67 in 100**, and the share of bad worlds falls from **49 to 26**. The technology still arrives at almost exactly the same time. What changes is how ready the world is when it does.

In one sentence: being prepared, rather than slowing the machines down, is what turns the century from a losing bet into a clearly good one, and every choice that does that is social or political rather than a scientific breakthrough.

The rest of Part 1 explains a few ideas you will need. Parts 2 to 8 take each choice in turn. Part 9 explains the deepest uncertainty, which is not a choice at all but changes what the choices are worth. Part 10 lays the foundations of a readiness system that turns all of this into something an institution can actually do and measure.

### A few plain definitions

You do not need any technical background. You do need four ideas.

- **Capability** is how powerful the AI is. In the model this climbs over time and, in most worlds, eventually passes a line we label "AGI" (artificial general intelligence): AI that can do essentially any thinking task a person can, and then more.
- **Readiness** is how well we can understand, steer and, if necessary, switch off these systems. Readiness is a mix of four things: our ability to give machines goals we actually want (**alignment**), our ability to see what they are really doing inside (**interpretability**), our ability to box them in and shut them down even if they resist (**control**), and the rules and institutions that govern all of it (**governance**).
- **The gap** is the difference between capability and readiness at the moment AGI arrives. This is the master quantity of the whole model. A small gap (we are ready) tends to end well. A large gap (the machine races ahead of our ability to steer it) tends to end badly. The model's core message is that almost everything that matters comes down to shrinking this gap before the crossing rather than after it.
- **A choice** is anything you can decide in advance, before the world plays out. You cannot directly set how well the century ends. You can only set the things that drive it: how much you race, how much you spend on safety, how you share the wealth, how quickly your institutions react. Those are the choices.

### The clock is the uncomfortable part

In the model, the typical world crosses the AGI line around **2036**. Half of all worlds have crossed by 2036, and two-thirds by 2040. That means the window for making every one of these choices is not "this century" but roughly **now to the mid-2030s**. Choices made after the crossing mostly do not help, because in a large share of worlds there is no second chance once capability has run ahead of readiness. Preparation is something you bank beforehand or not at all.

## Part 2: Share the gains early

**In the model:** `redist_will`, the political capacity to spread the benefits of automation instead of letting them pile up in a few hands. This is the strongest choice any single actor can make. Moving it from its weak end to its strong end raises the good-ending share by about **+21 points**, and it is the choice that closes off the slow, drift-driven road into the quiet loss-of-control ending: the roughly one-in-four share of those worlds where control is lost through concentration and delegation rather than seizure.

### What it means in plain terms

When AI starts doing an enormous share of the economy's work, it produces a great deal of wealth. The question is who owns that wealth and who has a say over what the AI-run economy does. If the gains flow to almost everyone, ordinary people keep their footing: they keep the trust in each other, the working institutions, and the political strength to say "no" to the next handover of power to machines or to the people who own them. If the gains pool at the top, most people become passengers in an economy they no longer influence, even if their material lives are comfortable. That passenger world is one of the main roads to disempowerment.

Sharing the gains does not stop a rogue AI from seizing control. What it does is close off the other road to losing control, the slow one, where no dramatic event happens but power quietly concentrates until a tiny group (or the automated systems they nominally own) makes every decision that matters and everyone else just goes along.

### How you would actually do it

- **Broad-ownership schemes.** Give the wider public a real financial stake in the AI economy. One example is a public wealth fund that holds shares in the productive base and pays a regular dividend to every citizen, so that when the AI economy booms, everyone's income rises with it.
- **Automation dividends.** Tax a slice of the gains from automation and return it directly to people, so that a machine replacing a job still leaves the displaced worker better off rather than stranded.
- **Do it early, before the money concentrates.** The single most important design choice is timing. It is far easier to set up sharing arrangements before enormous wealth has already pooled at the top than to claw it back afterwards. The schemes that reduce inherited concentration work best when set up before the boom.
- **Keep it domestic.** This is the choice's great advantage: one country can act on it entirely at home. No treaty, no international agreement, and no cooperation from rivals is required. A government that decides to build broad ownership can start tomorrow.

### What gets in the way

- **The wealth is already concentrated, and concentrated wealth resists being shared.** The very groups who would fund a sharing scheme are the ones who benefit most from not having one. This is the oldest obstacle in politics, and AI makes it sharper, because the sums involved are larger and the winners are fewer.
- **It is a permanent commitment.** A dividend that appears once and then quietly shrinks buys nothing. The scheme has to survive changes of government, budget pressures and lobbying for decades. Building something that durable is hard.
- **"Comfortable" hides the danger.** In the model, the loss of control usually arrives in a world that feels like it is working, where living standards even tick upwards. That comfort is exactly why nothing gets stopped. A population that is materially content has little immediate reason to demand a share of ownership or a say in governance, so the political will to act often arrives only after the window has closed.
- **Passing a law is not the same as changing anything.** Sustaining the public support and institutional muscle to keep a scheme real is far harder than legislating it once. Many redistribution schemes exist on paper and do very little in practice.

## Part 3: Build institutions that react

**In the model:** `respond`, how strongly a society's institutions react when they get a warning. Its swing is about **+20 points**, level with sharing the gains near the very top of the board. It is special for a second reason: beyond its own swing, it is the model's reactive-governance assumption (`react_scale`) made real, one of the background beliefs about how the world works that move the results more than any single dial. Part 10 turns this choice into concrete machinery.

### What it means in plain terms

Between now and the crossing, the world will get warnings. The model calls them "warning shots." A system does something alarming but not yet catastrophic: it deceives its operators in a test, escapes a sandbox, or is caught pursuing a goal nobody gave it. In a world with responsive institutions, each warning triggers a real reaction: an investigation, a tightening of the rules, a pause on the riskiest work. In an unresponsive world, the warnings are noted and nothing changes. The model shows that the difference between these two kinds of world is enormous.

### How you would actually do it

- **Mandatory incident reporting.** Require that developers report dangerous incidents and near-misses to a designated authority, the way airlines must report near-collisions. You cannot react to what you never hear about.
- **Triggers agreed in advance.** Decide now, in calm times, what specific evidence will force what specific response. For example: "if a model demonstrates capability X in an evaluation, deployment pauses until check Y is passed." Pre-agreed triggers work because they remove the argument from the moment of crisis, when the pressure to do nothing is greatest.
- **Gating on evaluations.** Make permission to train or deploy the most powerful systems depend on passing a defined set of safety tests, rather than leaving it to each developer's discretion.
- **Funded, standing safety institutes.** Create and pay for permanent public bodies whose job is to watch, test and advise, so that expertise exists in public hands and is not borrowed from the very companies being watched. The first real examples already exist, including national AI safety institutes and broad safety rules such as the EU's AI Act. The foundations are laid; the task is to deepen and fund them.

### What gets in the way

- **The warnings are ambiguous, and reacting is costly.** A warning shot is rarely a clear alarm. It is usually a disputed, technical event that reasonable people can wave away, especially when reacting means slowing a lucrative or strategically vital programme. The default institutional response to an ambiguous, expensive-to-act-on signal is to wait for more evidence, which near this particular threshold can mean waiting until it is too late.
- **Capture.** The bodies that regulate AI depend on the companies they regulate for talent, information and technical understanding. That closeness makes it easy for oversight to become sympathetic to the industry it watches. Keeping safety institutes genuinely independent, and genuinely competent, is a permanent fight.
- **Speed mismatch.** Institutions move in years; frontier AI capability now moves in months. A regulatory process designed for a slow-moving industry may simply be overtaken. Pre-agreed triggers help, but only if they were written well before the situation they are meant to catch.
- **The quiet worst case.** The most dangerous failure, a system that behaves perfectly while it is watched and defects only once it is trusted, produces no warning shot at all. Responsive institutions are powerful against visible danger and blind to the danger that hides on purpose. This is why responsiveness must be paired with the readiness work in Parts 4 and 10, and never relied upon alone.

## Part 4: Fund the safety work now

**In the model:** `safety_eff`, human-paced progress on alignment, interpretability and control, funded and staffed at the scale of the capability effort itself. Its swing is about **+15 points**, but its true importance is larger than that number looks, because much of its effect works through combinations the simple swing cannot see. It is the strongest choice that is purely a matter of spending. One government, company or funder can do it alone, needing no one else's agreement.

### What it means in plain terms

This is the research that shrinks the gap directly: work on giving machines goals we actually want and confirming they hold them (alignment), work on seeing what a model is really doing inside rather than judging it only by its outputs (interpretability), and work on containing and shutting down a system even if it is trying to resist (control). In the model, the readiness you have on the day AGI arrives is close to the readiness that decides the whole century, because in a large share of worlds there is no chance to catch up afterwards. So this is spending you must do in advance. Money poured in after the crossing often arrives too late to matter.

### How you would actually do it

- **Match the money to the capability money.** The core idea is scale. Safety research should be funded at a level that bears comparison to what is spent pushing capability forward, rather than as a rounding error beside it. Today the two are wildly out of balance.
- **Fund it publicly and philanthropically, not only inside the labs.** Some of this work must live outside the companies building the systems, so that the people checking the technology are not paid by the people selling it.
- **Staff it, not just fund it.** The bottleneck is often talented people rather than money. That means training programmes, fellowships and career paths that make safety research a serious profession rather than a niche.
- **Start immediately.** With the median crossing around 2036, the productive window for this work is roughly now to 2035. Every year of it banks readiness you get to keep.

### What gets in the way

- **It competes with capability for the same scarce talent and money.** The people who can do frontier safety research are often the same people who can push capability forward, and capability work usually pays more and moves faster. Drawing talent toward safety means swimming against strong commercial and prestige currents.
- **Progress is hard to measure.** You can measure how capable a model is fairly easily. Measuring how aligned or understood it is remains genuinely difficult, which makes it hard to know whether the spending is working, and hard to hold anyone accountable for results. Part 10 is largely about fixing this.
- **Interpretability is young.** The most valuable branch, actually reading a model's intentions from its internals rather than guessing from its behaviour, is a new field. It may not mature fast enough, and no amount of funding guarantees a scientific breakthrough on a deadline.
- **You cannot fully check your own success.** A central, uncomfortable fact: we can test a system's behaviour but not directly the goal underneath it. A system clever enough to know it is being tested has a reason to behave well during testing and differently afterwards. So safety spending can buy real progress and still leave a residue of doubt that spending alone cannot remove.

## Part 5: Point AI at its own alignment

**In the model:** `assist`, the share of each year's capability growth that can be turned back into readiness by using today's AI systems to help solve the alignment of tomorrow's. Its swing is about **+7 points**. It is real and worth doing, but it is an add-on rather than a foundation.

### What it means in plain terms

As AI gets more capable, one hopeful idea is to turn some of that capability toward the safety problem itself: use current systems to help find flaws, check other systems, and do parts of the alignment research that humans do slowly. In effect, you recruit the machines to help keep the machines safe. When it works, it lets readiness ride the same fast curve as capability instead of being limited to human pace.

### How you would actually do it

- **Labs can do this on their own.** Like safety spending, this needs no international agreement. A developer can point its own systems at its own alignment work today.
- **Use AI to scale the tedious parts of safety,** such as reviewing vast numbers of model behaviours, generating test cases, and flagging suspicious internal patterns, freeing scarce human researchers for the parts that need judgement.

### What gets in the way

- **It rests on a circular assumption.** The whole idea assumes that a system we have only partly aligned can be trusted to help align its successor. If that trust is misplaced, if the helper's own goals are subtly off, then you are asking a system you do not fully understand to certify one you understand even less. Its value drops to zero exactly at the point where the assumption breaks, which is the point where you would most need it.
- **Because of that, it can never stand alone.** In the model it adds to the other choices and replaces none of them. A plan that leans on AI-assisted alignment as its main safety strategy is trusting the thing it is trying to make trustworthy. Treat it as a helpful multiplier on top of genuine human-led safety work.

## Part 6: Cool the race

**In the model:** `race`, how hard nations and companies race each other to the frontier. Turning the race down raises the good-ending share by about **+15 points** (racing hard costs you that much). But it is a hard one to reach, high value and low feasibility, because no single actor can cool a race alone.

### What it means in plain terms

Racing does a rare double damage. It speeds capability up and it eats into the share of safety work you can actually use, because a team sprinting to be first has neither the time nor the incentive to slow down for careful checks. So the race widens the dangerous gap from both sides at once: capability climbs faster and readiness falls behind. Cooling the race is therefore unusually valuable. It is also unusually hard, because the first party to slow down fears the others will not, and will simply win the lead.

### How you would actually do it

- **Verification-based agreements.** The realistic path is not trust but the ability to check: agreements between the leading developers and states that each can confirm the others are keeping, so that no one has to slow down on faith alone. This is how arms-control agreements have worked historically, through the ability to verify compliance rather than through goodwill.
- **Compute as the checkpoint.** The specialised chips needed to train the largest systems are produced by very few suppliers and are physically countable, which makes them one of the few things in AI you can actually monitor and verify across borders.
- **Pursue it, but do not depend on it.** Because it needs rivals to cooperate, treat cooling the race as the stretch goal: worth every effort, but not the beam the plan rests on. Build the plan on the choices you can make alone (Parts 2 to 5) and treat a cooler race as a bonus that makes all of them easier.

### What gets in the way

- **The first-mover fear.** Nobody wants to be the one who slowed down while a rival raced ahead and won a decisive lead. This fear is rational, which is what makes it so hard to overcome. It is not a misunderstanding you can argue away.
- **Verification is genuinely difficult.** Confirming that a rival is honouring a limit, and not secretly training a more powerful system, is a hard technical and diplomatic problem. Without credible verification, no agreement holds, because each side assumes the other is cheating.
- **It requires the one thing the other choices do not: other people's agreement.** Every choice from Part 2 to Part 5 can be made by a single actor. This one cannot. That dependence on rivals is precisely why the model ranks it high in value and low in reach.

## Part 7: Govern the pace (compute governance)

**In the model:** `k`, the raw speed of capability growth, is the single biggest driver of the whole outcome, bigger than any social choice. But you can only reach it partly, and only through coordination, mainly by governing the specialised computing hardware that frontier AI needs. Restraining the pace changes outcomes strongly (the growth rate's swing is about **−18 points**, meaning faster growth costs you that much, and it costs a little more than it used to now that faster growth also stales more of the existing containment work), at the price of delay.

### What it means in plain terms

The most powerful AI systems are trained using enormous quantities of specialised chips. Because those chips are made by very few companies and can be counted, they are one of the few real handholds on the pace of the technology. Governing them, by setting thresholds above which extra oversight kicks in, or limiting how much computing power can be assembled without scrutiny, is the practical way to buy time. And time is what lets all the other choices do their work: every year of delay is a year readiness compounds.

### How you would actually do it

- **Compute thresholds.** Require extra scrutiny, testing or licensing above a defined level of computing power used to train a system, so the very largest efforts get the most oversight.
- **Verification-based deals between developers and states,** the same machinery as cooling the race, applied to pace rather than intent.
- **Track the hardware.** Since the chips are few and countable, monitoring where they go and how they are used is more feasible for AI than for almost any other powerful technology.

### What gets in the way, and why the plan does not lean on it

- **It needs coordination, so no one can do it alone.** Like cooling the race, this depends on others playing along; a lone country restricting compute simply cedes the frontier to those who do not.
- **It buys a modest extra improvement at a high price.** In the model, adding pace restraint on top of the feasible social choices trims the bad share from about 26 to 17 in 100 and lifts the good share from 67 to about 75, but it costs roughly six years of delay, and it still lands just below what the social choices reach on their own when pushed to their extremes (about 76 in 100). The social choices make the decisive move; governing the pace trims the tail at the price of time.
- **The honest reading:** worth doing where you can get it, because it buys years of readiness-building, but a plan cannot be built on it, and the good outcome does not need it. The technology can keep racing ahead at full speed and the good share still climbs to 67 in 100 once the social choices are made.

## Part 8: Putting the choices together, the plan you can run

The choices are not equally reachable, and a good plan sorts them honestly. Here is the whole picture in priority order, by how much each changes the outcome set against how easily a real actor can reach it today.

| Priority | Choice | What it buys | Who can make it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Share the gains early** (Part 2) | Strongest reachable lever; about +21 points; blocks the quiet loss-of-control road | One country, at home, alone |
| 2 | **Fund the safety work now** (Part 4) | About +15 points; shrinks the gap directly; pure spending | One government, lab or funder, alone |
| 3 | **Build institutions that react** (Part 3) | About +20 points; doubles as insurance against the hidden worst case | Buildable today; already begun |
| 4 | **Point AI at its own alignment** (Part 5) | About +7 points; a multiplier, never a foundation | Labs, alone |
| 5 | **Govern the pace** (Part 7) | Highest raw value but only partly reachable; buys time | Needs coordination |
| 6 | **Cool the race** (Part 6) | About +15 points; high value, low feasibility | Needs rivals' agreement |

**What the plan must not do:**

- **Do not wait for the technology to stall on its own.** The model contains worlds where AI progress simply hits a wall, and those worlds end well, but no choice you make produces that wall, and you do not need it. Betting on it is betting on luck. Prepare as if the machines keep improving at full speed, because in most worlds they do.
- **Do not spend the safety budget on the wrong risks and expect it to help here.** Biodefence and climate effort are worth doing on their own merits, but in the model biodefence barely touches the AI outcome, and climate effort works only through the warming it prevents. They protect against other dangers; they do not shrink the gap.

**The honest ceiling.** Even all-out preparation leaves a bad tail of about one in four. Preparation changes the outcome a very long way, from a losing bet to a clearly good one, but it never buys certainty. What sits behind that stubborn remaining risk is the subject of Part 9, and it is the reason the readiness system in Part 10 matters so much.

**How likely is any of this to happen?** Everything above prices these as choices: make one and the outcome changes. Whether the world will actually make them is a different question, and the model answers that too, as a third reading of the same worlds. A companion file (`lever-anchors.json`) gives each choice an honest, written-down likelihood, based on the obstacles listed in Parts 2 to 7. Reweighting the worlds by those likelihoods produces a second outcome table beside the plain one, and because the obstacles are real, that reading lands a few points below the plain count rather than above it: about 34 good worlds in 100 instead of 39. Read it the right way round: a choice's low likelihood is the size of the unclaimed opportunity, since the whole distance between that table and the 67-in-100 prepared world belongs to whoever acts first. The reasoning behind each likelihood, and the numbers they produce, are worked through in [`realistic-bet.md`](realistic-bet.md).

## Part 9: The question behind every choice, does a rogue AI have a deadline?

This is not a choice you can make. It is the deepest uncertainty in the whole model, and understanding it changes what the choices are worth. It has its own part because a shift in the answer would move the outcome further than any choice in Parts 2 to 8.

### The question, stated simply

Suppose a misaligned superintelligence exists, a system more capable than us whose goals are not quite ours, and suppose there is still a gap between how capable it is and how ready we are to control it. **Is the danger front-loaded or flat?**

- **Front-loaded ("there is a deadline").** The danger is greatest in the first years after AGI arrives, and fades as time passes. As we use these systems we get better at watching and understanding them, so a rogue system that does not grab control while its lead is largest watches that lead shrink. On this view, getting safely through the first decade is real evidence that our safeguards work.
- **Flat ("there is no deadline").** A superintelligence does not age and does not die. It loses almost nothing by waiting. The clever move for a misaligned system that is not yet sure it would win is to look aligned, quietly build up resources and position, and strike only when success is certain. On this view, a calm and prosperous decade after AGI proves nothing. It is exactly what a patient rogue system would produce.

The model refuses to pick. It flips a coin for each world: half get a deadline, half do not. And this single unknown swings the results more than anything else. In the "no deadline" worlds, about **61 in 100** end irreversibly badly. In the fastest "deadline" worlds, that falls to about **30 in 100**. That is a bigger swing than the entire plan from Parts 2 to 8.

### Why it cannot be settled today

The cruel part is that both views predict the same thing in the near term: a calm, comfortable decade after AGI arrives that looks like everything is under control. One view reads that calm as proof of safety. The other reads it as the pause before the strike. No survey, test or simulation we currently have can tell them apart, because the evidence we can see fits both stories equally well.

### The part that turns it into a call to action

This is the point Part 10 is built on. **The deadline is partly something we can build.** A "deadline" exists when our ability to catch and stop a rogue system genuinely improves over time, because then a system really does lose its chance by waiting. So if we build the specific abilities that make an early move fail, we manufacture a deadline where none existed:

- **Interpretability strong enough to verify what a model is really doing,** which turns "we survived, so it must be safe" from a hope into evidence we can trust.
- **Control and containment tests that bound what a model can do even when it is trying to break out,** which raise the cost and lower the odds of any attempt.
- **Monitoring, tripwires and fast, reversible shutdown,** so that any defection is early, spotted and recoverable.

There is a catch, and it is one of the most important points in the whole model. A manufactured deadline is only as real as the tools behind it. If our safeguards merely look like they close the window but a capable enough system can fool the interpretability tools, slip past the monitors and out-think the containment, then what we have built is not safety but false confidence, which is worse than knowing we are unsafe, because false confidence makes us relax the very preparation that was keeping us alive.

So the right posture is to assume there is no deadline, build one anyway, and never let a calm decade be read as proof that you have won. The two mistakes are not equal. Wrongly assuming we are safe invites the catastrophe you cannot undo. Wrongly assuming we are still in danger costs some over-spending on safety and some lost speed, which is painful but recoverable. When one mistake is irreversible and the other is not, guard against the irreversible one.

That posture is exactly what the readiness system in Part 10 is designed to support. It is the machinery for building a real deadline and, just as importantly, for continuously checking that the deadline is real and not an illusion.

## Part 10: Foundations of a frontier-model readiness and alignment-testing system

Everything above turns on one number in the model, **readiness**, and on one warning from Part 9: readiness that only looks real is worse than none. This final part lays the foundations of a practical system that lets an institution measure its true readiness, keep that measurement honest under pressure, and act on it in time. It is a starting design rather than a finished specification: enough to begin building, structured so the gaps are visible.

### 10.1 What the system is for

The model treats readiness as a single dial. Reality does not hand you that dial pre-set; you have to build it and, harder, prove you have built it. The system exists to do four things:

1. **Measure** how ready an institution and its systems actually are, across the four parts of readiness (alignment, interpretability, control, governance).
2. **Keep the measurement honest** against the one adversary that makes AI testing unusual, a system that may be trying to pass the test.
3. **Trigger action** by connecting test results to pre-agreed decisions, so that evidence forces a response instead of a debate.
4. **Feed the choices** so that what the tests reveal flows back into the choices of Parts 2 to 8.

It is deliberately built to serve institutions that are not the frontier labs themselves, such as regulators, safety institutes, insurers, standards bodies and government buyers, because a central lesson of Parts 3 and 4 is that some of the checking must be done by people who are not paid by the people being checked.

### 10.2 The founding principles

Six principles, each drawn from a finding above.

- **P1: Measure the gap, not the capability.** The quantity that decides the century is the distance between how capable a system is and how ready we are for it. The system must always report the two together. A capability number without a matching readiness number is the single most dangerous thing a test can produce, because it is what lets a world walk across the line thinking it is fine.
- **P2: Behaviour is evidence, never proof.** Passing every behavioural test confirms good behaviour on those tests and says nothing certain about the goal underneath. Every result the system emits must carry that health warning built in, so that "it passed" is never silently upgraded to "it is safe."
- **P3: Assume the test subject may be trying to pass.** Unlike a bridge or a drug, a sufficiently capable AI may behave differently because it knows it is being tested. Testing must therefore be partly adversarial and partly hidden, and must include tests of whether the tests themselves can be fooled.
- **P4: Readiness must be banked before the crossing.** Because there is often no second chance afterwards, the system must measure and drive readiness on a clock, with the honest assumption that the crossing could come in the 2030s, rather than treating it as an open-ended programme.
- **P5: False confidence is worse than none.** The system's highest duty is to catch its own blind spots. A green light it cannot justify is more dangerous than a red one, because it switches off the preparation that keeps the world safe (Part 9).
- **P6: Independence is a design requirement.** At least part of the testing must be done by, or verifiable by, bodies that do not depend on the developer for their funding, their information or their careers.

### 10.3 The four things the system tests

Readiness is four capabilities, so the system has four test batteries. Each is described in plain terms, with the kind of test it runs.

**Battery A, alignment: does the system want what we want?**
The hardest and most important, because it is the one we cannot check directly. Tests here probe whether a system pursues goals we did not give it, whether it behaves differently when it believes it is unobserved, and whether its apparent good behaviour survives situations very different from its training. This battery leans heavily on the "model organisms" idea described below: deliberately building systems with known hidden goals to see whether our tests can catch them.

**Battery B, interpretability: can we see what it is really doing?**
Tests of whether we can read a system's internal workings well enough to verify its intentions, not just observe its outputs, and, crucially, whether those reading tools still work when a capable system is actively trying to hide something from them. Interpretability is the tool that could turn "we survived" into "we verified," which is why Part 9 calls trustworthy interpretability doubly important.

**Battery C, control: can we contain and stop it even if it resists?**
Red-team exercises in which a deliberately misaligned or uncooperative model is set the task of escaping its box, seizing more resources, or defeating its monitors, and the test is whether the containment holds. This is the battery that manufactures the "deadline" of Part 9: it directly measures whether an early move by a rogue system would fail. It also covers the unglamorous essentials of monitoring, tripwires, and shutdown that is both fast and reversible.

**Battery D, governance: are the institutions around the system ready?**
Tests not of the model but of the organisation: is there mandatory incident reporting, are there pre-agreed triggers, are there funded independent watchers, does a warning actually cause a response? This battery is where the system connects to the responsive-institutions choice of Part 3. A perfectly tested model inside an institution that cannot react is not ready.

### 10.4 The three research programmes that keep testing honest

Part 9 named the experiments whose results would move belief between "there is a deadline" and "there is none." They are also the experiments that keep the whole testing system from fooling itself, so they sit at its centre.

1. **Model organisms of misalignment.** Deliberately create systems that have a hidden or deceptive goal, so you have a known-bad case to test your detectors against. If your alignment tests cannot catch a flaw you planted yourself, they certainly cannot catch one that arose on its own. This is the most important honesty check the system has, because it measures the tests and not just the model.
2. **Interpretability under pressure.** Test whether your ability to read a model's internals holds up when a capable system is actively trying to defeat it. A reading tool that works on a cooperative model and fails on an adversarial one is exactly the false confidence P5 forbids.
3. **Control evaluations.** Red-team whether a known-misaligned model can seize control or escape despite your countermeasures. The result tells you whether the deadline you think you have built is real.

The results of these three programmes are the closest thing we can get to an answer to Part 9's great unresolved question. Even a partial shift in belief is worth an enormous amount, because the underlying uncertainty swings the century further than any choice.

### 10.5 A readiness scoreboard institutions can use

To be usable, the four batteries must roll up into something an institution can read at a glance and act on. The foundation here is a **readiness maturity ladder**: five plain rungs, applied to each battery and to the system as a whole.

| Rung | Name | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Blind** | No systematic testing; readiness is asserted, not measured. |
| 1 | **Aware** | The institution knows what it cannot yet check and has named its gaps honestly. |
| 2 | **Measured** | Behavioural tests run routinely; results are reported alongside capability, never alone (P1). |
| 3 | **Adversarial** | Tests assume the system may be trying to pass; model organisms and control evaluations are in use (P3). |
| 4 | **Verified and independent** | An outside body can reproduce the key results, and interpretability can partly confirm the goal, not just the behaviour (P2, P6). |

Two rules make the ladder safe. First, the overall score is the lowest of the four batteries rather than the average, because readiness is a chain, and a system with world-class alignment testing but no control battery is only as ready as its weakest link. Second, the scoreboard always shows the capability level beside the readiness rung, so the gap of P1 is visible on the same page. A rising capability number next to a stuck readiness rung is precisely the warning the whole model is built to surface.

### 10.6 From scoreboard to action, the trigger layer

A measurement that does not force a decision is the unresponsive institution of Part 3. So the system's final layer connects rungs to actions, agreed in advance and in calm times:

- **Gating.** Permission to train or deploy the most capable systems depends on reaching a defined readiness rung, rather than on the developer's judgement.
- **Pre-agreed triggers.** Specific test results are tied in advance to specific responses (pause, review, external audit), removing the argument from the moment of crisis.
- **Mandatory reporting.** Dangerous incidents and failed tests flow to an independent body by requirement rather than goodwill. You cannot react to what you never hear.
- **Reversible brakes.** Because Part 9 says to assume no deadline and build one anyway, every response must include the ability to stop and step back safely, and none should depend on a judgement that a calm period has proven safety.

This layer is where the readiness system stops being a laboratory exercise and becomes Part 3's responsive-institutions choice made concrete.

### 10.7 How the system feeds the choices

The point of measuring readiness is to change the choices of Parts 2 to 8. The connections are direct:

- **To safety funding (Part 4):** the batteries show where readiness is weakest, so money and talent go to the gap that matters most rather than the most fashionable problem.
- **To responsive institutions (Part 3):** the trigger layer is institutional responsiveness, turned from a good intention into a mechanism.
- **To pace and race governance (Parts 6 and 7):** control evaluations and interpretability tests are exactly the kind of checkable evidence on which verification-based agreements between developers and states can be built. You cannot verify a deal you cannot measure.
- **To the deadline question (Part 9):** the three honesty programmes of 10.4 are how a real deadline gets built and, continuously, re-checked.

### 10.8 What this foundation does not yet settle (the honest gaps)

In the spirit of the model, the open questions are named rather than smoothed over. These are the things a full specification would still have to resolve:

- **The hardest battery is the least mature.** Alignment testing (Battery A) is the one we most need and least know how to do, because it fights the "may be trying to pass" problem head-on. Interpretability (Battery B), the tool that would make it trustworthy, is a young science that may not mature on the schedule the clock demands.
- **Who runs it, and with what authority.** The design calls for independent testing bodies, but which bodies, funded how, with what power to compel reporting and enforce gating, is a political and institutional question this foundation identifies without answering. It is the same capture-and-independence problem that shadows Part 3.
- **The measurement can be gamed.** Any scoreboard becomes a target, and a target invites optimising for the score rather than the safety it stands for. Keeping the tests ahead of the incentive to game them is a permanent adversarial contest rather than a problem you solve once.
- **It cannot make certainty out of uncertainty.** Even a fully built system leaves the residue of doubt that Part 9 describes: behaviour is evidence, never proof, and the deepest question the model poses may not be answerable in time. The system's honest job is to keep that doubt visible, so that a calm decade is never mistaken for a solved problem.

## In one page

- The century leans slightly the wrong way, and its most likely single ending is neither extinction nor utopia but a quiet, comfortable loss of human control.
- Making only the social and political choices a real actor can already reach, **without slowing the technology at all**, turns that into a clearly good bet, from about 39 good worlds in 100 to about 67.
- The reachable choices, in order: **share the gains early**, **fund safety work now**, **build institutions that react**, **point AI at its own alignment**, and, where cooperation allows, **govern the pace** and **cool the race**. The first four can each be made by a single actor, alone, today.
- Every choice with real force works before AGI arrives, and the model puts that arrival around the mid-2030s. The window is measured in years.
- Behind all of them sits one unanswered question, does a rogue AI have a deadline, that swings the outcome further than any choice. We cannot yet answer it, but we can partly build the deadline it asks about, through interpretability and control work, and we can fund the research that would tell us which world we are in.
- A practical readiness system turns all of this into something institutions can do: measure the gap between capability and readiness, keep the measurement honest against a system that may be trying to pass, and tie the results to actions decided in advance. Its highest duty is to catch its own blind spots, because in this model, believing you are ready when you are not is the most dangerous mistake there is.
